About me

Bridging psychology & data

I'm a Psychology student at Dartmouth College with a deep interest in mental health, social connection, and how data can illuminate human experience. My work sits at the intersection of quantitative research methods and the very human questions of belonging and wellbeing.

The healthy-minds-loneliness project grew out of a desire to understand what the data can tell us about how college students experience loneliness — and how belonging and stigma shape that relationship with mental health in ways that are more complex than they might first appear.

The project

Healthy Minds & Loneliness

This research project examines the relationship between loneliness, campus belonging, mental health stigma, and mental health outcomes — specifically depression and anxiety — among college students.

📋

Data source: The Healthy Minds Study (HMS)

This project uses data from the Healthy Minds Study, a large-scale annual survey of college student mental health administered by the Healthy Minds Network — a research consortium led by Boston University, University of Michigan, UCLA, and Wayne State University. Over 935,000 responses have been gathered from students at more than 675 colleges and universities, making HMS one of the most comprehensive datasets on post-secondary student mental health available. It measures depression, anxiety, help-seeking behavior, and a range of social and psychological variables.

🔍

Research question

How does loneliness predict depression and anxiety in college students, and do campus belonging and stigma shape this relationship?

📊

Methods

Quantitative analysis using validated screening tools including PHQ-9 (depression) and GAD-7 (anxiety), alongside loneliness, belonging, and stigma scales from the HMS dataset.

🏷️

Stigma as a variable

Mental health stigma — the social shame and fear of judgment around seeking help — is measured as a key variable. Stigma may worsen the effects of loneliness by discouraging students from reaching out for support when they need it most.

🎓

Population

College student respondents across demographic groups, allowing for subgroup analysis by identity and experience.

💡

Significance

Findings suggest concrete, intervention-relevant insights — reducing stigma and strengthening campus belonging may both be levers for improving student mental health outcomes.

Key findings

What the data shows

Loneliness is the strongest independent predictor of both depression and anxiety among emerging adults — even after controlling for identity, belonging, stigma, and age. Depression and anxiety are strongly correlated with each other (r = 0.74), and both correlate meaningfully with loneliness (r = 0.54 and r = 0.47, respectively).

Stigma amplifies the harm: perceived stigma significantly strengthens the loneliness–depression relationship, while campus belonging reduces depression independently — though it does not buffer the loneliness pathway itself. Sexual minority students bear a disproportionate burden, reporting nearly a full point higher loneliness than heterosexual peers (Cohen's d = 0.47).

Figure 1 — Depression & anxiety rise with loneliness severity

Mean PHQ-9 (depression) and GAD-7 (anxiety) scores by loneliness level. The red dashed line marks the clinical screening threshold (≥ 10). Students in the "quite lonely" to "extremely lonely" range exceed clinical thresholds on average. Data: HMS 2024–25, emerging adults aged 18–24 (n = 61,288).

Figure 2 — Sexual minority students report significantly higher loneliness

Mean UCLA-3 loneliness score by sexual orientation. Sexual minority students average 6.15 vs. 5.25 for heterosexual peers — crossing the loneliness threshold (≥ 6). Cohen's d = 0.47, a moderate effect. Racial differences were negligible (d = 0.05). Data: HMS 2024–25, emerging adults aged 18–24.

Key takeaways

  • 44.2% of emerging adults meet the loneliness threshold; 31.6% screen positive for depression, 28.8% for anxiety
  • Loneliness predicts depression (β = 1.26) and anxiety (β = 1.09) after controlling for all other factors
  • Perceived stigma significantly amplifies the loneliness–depression relationship
  • Campus belonging reduces depression independently but does not buffer the loneliness pathway
  • Sexual minority students report ~1 point higher loneliness than heterosexual peers (d = 0.47)
